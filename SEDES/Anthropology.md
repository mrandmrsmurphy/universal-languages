# Anthropology

> Parent: [[SEDES]]

**SEDES** (Somalia, Eritrea, Djibouti, Ethiopia, Sudan; native name ቀርኒ, *q'arnī*) is the Universal Language for the Horn of Africa. The languages of the region are legion, but they have long formed a single sprachbund (see the [Ethiopian language area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_language_area)). About 117 million people live there, and the language is written in Geʿez for now; eventually the script invented by Bakri Sapalo will be used.

## Design

SEDES has a Cushitic–Ethiosemitic centre of gravity: regional in sentence structure and vocabulary, but much more regular in morphology. It is not simplified Amharic, reconstructed Afroasiatic, or Esperanto with Ethiopian words.

The foundation is the Horn of Africa sprachbund. Proto-Afroasiatic is consulted, but recognition comes first: where no candidate clearly wins, a pseudo-historical proto-SEDES root may be built.

The core constituency is the speakers of Somali, Oromo, Amharic, Tigrinya, Afar, Saho and Tigre. The net is cast as wide as the sources allow, with smaller Ethiopian, Eritrean and other language communities included in testing, and it can be narrowed later. A language optimised for the Ethiopian highlands would look rather different from one intended equally for coastal Somalia.

Morphology is moderately agglutinative. The speakers' own languages are mostly fusional, but they differ so much in method that agglutinative is an acceptable compromise, in the same way as in Esperanto.

## Shared features

The most useful starting point is the regional convergence in grammar, not a presumed common ancestry. SOV order, postpositions, subordinate clauses before main clauses, and extensive use of converbs recur across the area, though no language shares every feature.

- Ejectives: pʼ, tʼ, čʼ, kʼ
- Long and short vowels
- Gemination
- SOV, mostly head-final
- Suffixing cases, including definiteness and focus
- Converbs
- Light-verb constructions with "say" and "do"

## Vocabulary

Words are chosen by recognition, not by equal slices from each donor language. A comparative database with entries from at least the seven core languages is evaluated by:

1. recognition across independent speaker groups;
2. frequency and usefulness;
3. ease of pronunciation;
4. distinctiveness from existing words;
5. cultural and political acceptability;
6. compatibility with regular derivation.

A zonal recognition language seeks forms people can already recognize, while a reconstructed common language seeks historically defensible ancestral forms; for an auxiliary language the first goal wins. Patchwork forms, such as the first syllable of an Oromo word joined to the last syllable of an Amharic word, are avoided unless testing shows they help. Pronouns, numerals, negation and other high-frequency grammatical words are tested together as whole systems, since they must be easy to tell apart in rapid speech.
