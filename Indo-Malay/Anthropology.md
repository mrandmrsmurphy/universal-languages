# Anthropology

> Parent: [[Indo-Malay]]

![[Indo-Malay_flag.png\|180]]

Indo-Malay is the Universal Language for Maritime Southeast Asia — the island world from Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula through Java, Borneo, Sulawesi and the Lesser Sundas to the Philippines. Its base is Proto-Malayo-Polynesian with modifications, and its zone covers roughly 380 million people in Indonesia, Malaysia, Brunei, Singapore, East Timor and the Philippines.

## Languages

The zone is almost entirely Austronesian, and the languages fall into a few blocs.

- **Malayic.** Malay, Indonesian, Minangkabau and their relatives. Malay has been the trade language of the archipelago for centuries; Indonesian, its standardised national form, is the first or second language of most of Indonesia's population, and Malaysian Malay, Bruneian Malay and Singapore Malay are national or official languages in their states.
- **Javanese and Sundanese.** Javanese is the largest regional language of the zone, with around 80 million speakers, and has a rich literary and courtly-register tradition; Sundanese has about 40 million. Both have speech levels that mark politeness.
- **Bali-Sasak and Sumatran.** Balinese, with its caste-sensitive speech levels, and the Batak, Acehnese and Lampung languages of Sumatra.
- **South Sulawesi.** Bugis and Makassar, the languages of the Lontara script that Indo-Malay adopts. Bugis has about 5 million speakers, Makassar about 2 million.
- **Philippine.** Tagalog (the base of the national language Filipino), Cebuano, Ilocano, Hiligaynon, Kapampangan, Pangasinan, Bikol, Waray, Tausug, Maranao and others. The Philippine languages differ from the Indonesian bloc in verb-initial word order and voice-marking verbs, and all of them are in the zone.

Malagasy, Chamorro, the Austronesian languages of Taiwan and the Oceanic languages lie outside the zone, though they can draw on Indo-Malay. Malay-speaking Southern Thailand is a border area shared with [[MSEAL]].

## Lingua franca and contact

Malay has served as the lingua franca of the region since the early maritime kingdoms and the Strait of Malacca trade. Layers of contact have left vocabulary from Sanskrit, Arabic, Chinese (especially Hokkien), Portuguese, Spanish (in the Philippines), Dutch and English. Indonesian was chosen as the national language of Indonesia over the much more widely spoken Javanese, partly to avoid privileging the largest ethnic group; the same neutrality argument underlies Indo-Malay.
