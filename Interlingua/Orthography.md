# Orthography

> Parent: [[Interlingua]] · Sounds: [[Interlingua/Phonology]]

Interlingua uses the conventional twenty-six letters of the Roman alphabet, with no diacritics and no signs for stress. The spelling is largely phonemic. Letter names:

| a | be | ce | de | e | ef | ge | ha | i | jota | ka | el | em |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| en | o | pe | cu | er | es | te | u | ve | duple ve | ix | ypsilon | zeta |

For sound values, see [[Interlingua/Phonology]]. The norm of pronunciation is "continental," fluid within type limits; native habits are allowed to prevail in intonation and sound duration.

## Written double consonants

Double consonants are written but merge in pronunciation. ss is always voiceless (as in *miss*). cc is spelled c at the end of a word: siccar but sic.

## Guest words

Unassimilated foreign words keep the spelling and pronunciation of their language of origin. Diacritics are omitted when the simplified spelling still suggests the pronunciation: defaite (French défaite), but kümmel (German).

## Capitalization

Upper-case initials occur only with proper names — not with derivatives from them, unlike English:

> In Francia le franceses parla francese ab le initio de lor vita.
> Le ver stilo shakespearean se trova solmente in Shakespeare.

Sacred terms, holidays, and names of movements, eras, and doctrines may be treated as proper or common names depending on the meaning intended: le romanticismo de Hollywood, but le Romanticismo (the movement).

## Collateral orthography

Grammar §15 defines a simplified **collateral orthography** of equal standing with the standard one (the Interlingua–English Dictionary does not observe it; unassimilated guest words are exempt). Its rules:

- Double letters for a single consonant are simplified, except ss — and except cc before e, i, y, which is two consonants: eclesia, aliterar, aducer, interogar (for ecclesia, alliterar, adducer, interrogar), but massa, accidente.
- Vowel **y** becomes i: tirano (for tyranno). Semiconsonant y stays: yak.
- **ph** becomes f: fonetic, emfatic.
- **ch** for /k/ is kept only before e and i: cloric, Cristo, but chimeric.
- Silent **h** after r and t is dropped: retoric, patetic.
- **j** replaces g and gi for /ʒ/: sajo (for sagio).
- **-age** becomes -aje (suffix or not): saje, coraje (for sage, corage).
- **-isar** becomes -izar, with z in its derivatives: civilizar, civilization.
- Final **-e** is dropped after t preceded by a vowel, unless the stress is third-from-last: animat, brevitat, but composite. The same after n, l, r when they stand for nn, ll, rr: peren, bel, mel, il, bizar. Present-tense and imperative forms keep their -e (pote, permite).

## Punctuation

Punctuation follows the rhythm of the spoken sentence; the signs and their values are as in English. In enumerations, a comma is kept even before e 'and' or o 'or': le asino, le can, e le catto.
