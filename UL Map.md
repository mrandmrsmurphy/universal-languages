# UL Map

> Hub: [[Universal Languages]] · Compare: [[Language Comparisons]]

This map shows which Universal Language would serve each region of the world. Each colour is one language, and the grey and blank areas are not covered by any of the fifteen. Regional boundaries within a country would vary.

![[Universal_Language_cb.png|1000]]

## [[Dan'a'yo]]

On the world map you can see that Dan'a'yo would serve East Asia: China, Japan and Korea, with a strip of Vietnam. It is the largest of the fifteen, with about 1.5 billion speakers.

![[Far_East_Asia.png|700]]

This closer view shows the East Asian cultural sphere, the lands shaped by Classical Chinese writing, which is the foundation Dan'a'yo draws on.

![[East_Asian_Cultural_Sphere.png|450]]

## [[Neo-Sanskrit]]

Neo-Sanskrit would serve the Indo-Aryan countries of South Asia: Pakistan, India, Nepal and Bangladesh, with Sri Lanka also marked. The Dravidian languages of the far south of India belong to Dravindian instead.

![[Indo-Aryan_world.png|700]]

This map shows the Indo-Aryan languages of the subcontinent in detail, from the northwest to Bengal, and Sinhala in Sri Lanka. Regional boundaries blur at the edges, and speakers often live well outside their language's core area.

![[Neo_Sanskrit.png|420]]

## [[Interlingua]]

Interlingua would serve the Romance-speaking world: Spain, Portugal, France, Italy and Romania in Europe, Latin America, Quebec, and parts of Africa where French or Portuguese is used. Darker and lighter shades show how central a Romance language is in each place.

![[Detailed_SVG_map_of_the_Romance-speaking_world.png|700]]

In Europe, the local map breaks the Romance family into its languages and dialects, from Portuguese and Galician in the west to Romanian in the east. Regional boundaries would vary.

![[Romance_languages.png|450]]

## [[Folksprak]]

Folksprak would serve the Germanic-speaking world, which includes the English-speaking countries: the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand, together with Scandinavia, the Netherlands, Germany and Austria. Lighter shades mark places in Africa and South Asia where a Germanic language, usually English, is widely used.

![[Germanic_languages.png|700]]

This map shows the Germanic languages of Europe in detail: Icelandic and Faroese, the Scandinavian languages, English and Scots, Frisian, Dutch, Low German and High German. The dialects shade into one another, so boundaries are approximate.

![[Germanic_languages_with_dialects_revised.png|450]]

## [[Kintu]]

Kintu would serve Bantu-speaking Africa, the southern half of the continent from the Congo basin and the Great Lakes down to South Africa. It covers about 450 million people.

![[Big_Bantu_map.png|700]]

The local map outlines the Bantu languages (shaded pink) within the wider Niger-Congo family. Fewer than a fifth of the languages could be labelled, so this shows the shape of the region rather than every language in it.

![[Map_of_the_Bantu_languages.png|450]]

## [[Indo-Malay]]

Indo-Malay would serve the island world of Southeast Asia: Malaysia, Indonesia and the Philippines. It is a region of thousands of islands, but its languages are closely related.

![[IM_world.png|700]]

This map shows the same region more closely: the area botanists call Malesia, from the Malay Peninsula to the Philippines and Indonesia. Regional boundaries would vary, especially at the eastern edge.

![[WGSRPD_Malesia.png|450]]

## [[Guosa]]

Guosa would serve a broad band of West Africa and the Sahel, from Mauritania and Senegal east through Mali, Niger and Nigeria to Chad.

![[Guosa.png|700]]

This language map of West Africa shows how many languages share the region, grouped by family (Mande, Atlantic, Kwa, Gur and others). It is a useful reminder that Guosa has to bridge many different local languages.

![[Carta_linguistica_dell'Africa_Occidentale.png|450]]

## [[Interslavic]]

Interslavic would serve the Slavic world: Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Poland, Czechia and Slovakia, and the Slavic-speaking Balkans, which are shown here as part of the audience.

![[Slavic_world.png|700]]

This closer map shows the three branches of Slavic Europe: Eastern (dark green), Western (light green) and Southern (dark teal). The Southern Slavic countries are an audience for Interslavic but vote in Balkan.

![[Slavic_europe_(Kosovo_unshaded).png|450]]

## [[Middle Semitic]]

Middle Semitic would serve the Arabic-speaking heartland: North Africa from Morocco to Egypt, the Levant, Iraq and the Arabian Peninsula.

![[Semitic_world.png|700]]

This map shows the varieties of Arabic across the region. The pale areas are mostly desert with few speakers, and the boundaries between varieties vary.

![[Semitic_map.png|450]]

## [[MSEAL]]

MSEAL would serve mainland Southeast Asia: Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam, together with the neighbouring borderlands.

![[MSEAL_world.png|700]]

The local map shows the ethnolinguistic groups of the mainland: Tai, Tibeto-Burman, Austroasiatic and others. They belong to several different families, which is why MSEAL is a sprachbund, a shared language area, rather than a single family.

![[Ethnolinguistic_Groups_of_Mainland_Southeast_Asia.png|360]]

## [[Dravindian]]

You can see that Dravindian would serve a compact region of southern India, with a small outlier in Pakistan. This map is an approximate distribution from around 1950.

![[World_Dravidian.png|700]]

This map shows the Dravidian subgroups: Telugu, Kannada, Tamil and Malayalam in the south, smaller languages such as Gondi and Kui further north, and Brahui in Pakistan. Boundaries are approximate.

![[Dravidian_subgroups.png|420]]

## [[Zens]]

Zens would serve the Iranian-speaking world: Iran, Afghanistan and Tajikistan, together with Kurdish and Baloch lands in neighbouring countries.

![[Zens_world.png|700]]

The local map shows the Sasanian Empire at its height, the historic Iranian world that Zens looks back to. Its speakers today include Persian, Pashto, Kurdish and Balochi speakers.

![[The_Sasanian_Empire_at_its_apex_under_Khosrow_II.png|450]]

## [[Ortatürk]]

Ortatürk serves the Turkic world, from Turkey across Azerbaijan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, with Turkic regions in China and Siberia shown in lighter blues.

![[Map-TurkicLanguages.png|700]]

This map shows the Turkic languages across Eurasia: Turkish in Anatolia, the Central Asian languages, and the scattered Turkic peoples of Siberia. The areas overlap, so regional boundaries would vary.

![[Turkic_Languages_distribution_map.png|450]]

## [[SEDES]]

SEDES would serve the Horn of Africa, a small region you can see on the world map: Somalia, Eritrea, Djibouti, Ethiopia and Sudan, which give the language its name.

![[Horn_world.png|700]]

This closer map shows the language families of the region: Semitic (such as Amharic and Tigrinya), Cushitic (such as Oromo and Somali) and Omotic, which share the area and whose speakers often live side by side.

![[Horn_Afroasiatic.png|420]]

## [[Balkan]]

Balkan would serve the Balkan peninsula, centred on Greece, Albania, Bulgaria and North Macedonia and their neighbours. It is the smallest of the fifteen, with about 60 million speakers.

![[Balkan_world.png|700]]

This map shows the languages of the Balkan sprachbund: Romanian, Bulgarian, Albanian, Greek, Turkish and others. Their areas overlap heavily, which is exactly why a shared language makes sense here.

![[Languages_of_the_Balkan_Sprachbund_in_the_Balkans,_Cyprus_and_Italy.png|450]]
